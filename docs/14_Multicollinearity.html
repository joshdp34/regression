14  Multicollinearity and Principal Component Regression

“The greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see.” - John Tukey

14.1 Multicollinearity

Often, two or more of the independent variables used in the model for \(E(y)\) will contribute redundant information. That is, the independent variables will be correlated with each other.

For example, suppose we want to construct a model to predict the gasoline mileage rating, \(y\), of a truck as a function of its load, \(x_1\), and the horsepower, \(x_2\), of its engine.

In general, you would expect heavier loads to require greater horsepower and to result in lower mileage ratings.

Thus, although both \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) contribute information for the prediction of mileage rating, some of the information is overlapping, because \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) are correlated.

When the independent variables are correlated, we say that multicollinearity exists.

The ability to obtain a good fit or to make inferences on the mean response or to predict the response are not affected by multicollinearity. However, inferences for the coefficients (the \(\beta\)s) and for the model variance (\(\sigma^2\)) are affected by large correlation among the predictor variables.

NoteWhat multicollinearity does and does not do

Multicollinearity is mainly a problem for interpreting individual regression coefficients.

Goal Is multicollinearity a major problem? Why?
Predicting new responses Usually no Correlated predictors can still combine to produce good predictions.
Estimating the mean response Usually no The fitted mean may still be estimated well in regions supported by the data.
Testing individual coefficients Yes Standard errors can become inflated, making individual predictors look nonsignificant.
Interpreting partial slopes Yes Holding one predictor fixed while changing another may be unrealistic when predictors move together.
Deciding which variable is “important” Yes Correlated predictors share information, so their unique contributions are hard to separate.

The awkward part is that a model can predict well while still being difficult to interpret.

Another effect of multicollinearity is the interpretation of the estimated coefficients. In multiple regression, we interpret the coefficient as the average change in \(y\) when \(x\) is increased by one unit when all other predictor variables are held constant.

If \(x\) is highly correlated with one or more of the other predictor variables, then it may not be feasible to think of varying \(x\) when the others are constant.

14.1.1 Variance Inflation Factors

We can see evidence of multicollinearity by examining the scatterplot matrix since this will give us a plot of each pair of predictor variables. If there are pairs that appear to be highly correlated (ggpairs in R will give the correlation value as well) then multicollinearity will be present.

We could also examine \(R_{a}^{2}\) for models with and without certain pairs of variables. If \(R_{a}^{2}\) decreases when a particular \(x\) variable is added but it appears to have a strong linear relationship with \(y\) in the scatterplot matrix, then this is evidence of multicollinearity.

A more convenient way to examine multicollinearity is through the use of the variance inflation factors (VIF).

Each predictor variable will have a VIF. Suppose we are interested in the VIF for \(x_{1}\). We start by regressing \(x_{1}\) on all the other predictor variables. Thus, we fit the model \[ \begin{align*} x_{i1} & =\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{2}x_{i2}+\alpha_{3}x_{i3}+\cdots+\alpha_{p-1}x_{i,p-1}+\epsilon \end{align*} \] where the \(\alpha\)’s are the coefficients and \(\epsilon\) is the random error term.

Now find the coefficient of multiple determination for this model which we will denote as \(R_{1}^{2}\). The VIF for \(x_{1}\) is then \[ \begin{align*} VIF_{1} & =\frac{1}{1-R_{1}^{2}}. \end{align*} \] We can do this for any \(i\)th predictor variable so that the VIF for that variable is \[ \begin{align} VIF_{i} & =\frac{1}{1-R_{i}^{2}} \end{align} \tag{14.1}\] where \(R_{i}^{2}\) is the coefficient of multiple determination for the regression fit of \(x_{i}\) on all the other predictor variables.

A rule of thumb is that a VIF greater than 10 is evidence that multicollinearity is high when that variable is added to the model. Some use a cutoff of 5 instead of 10.

ExampleWorked example: Interpreting VIF values

The VIF for predictor \(x_i\) is

\[ VIF_i=\frac{1}{1-R_i^2}, \]

where \(R_i^2\) comes from regressing \(x_i\) on the other predictors. As \(R_i^2\) gets close to 1, the VIF grows quickly.

vif_interpretation <- tibble::tibble(
  r_squared_from_other_predictors = c(0, 0.50, 0.80, 0.90, 0.95, 0.99),
  vif = 1 / (1 - r_squared_from_other_predictors)
)

knitr::kable(vif_interpretation, digits = 2)

A VIF of 10 means the estimated variance of a coefficient is 10 times as large as it would be if that predictor were uncorrelated with the other predictors, all else equal. Since standard errors are square roots of variances, this corresponds to a standard error about \(\sqrt{10}\approx 3.16\) times as large.

TipWhat should we do when VIFs are high?

A high VIF is a diagnostic, not an automatic instruction to delete a variable. The next step depends on the purpose of the model.

Goal Reasonable response Caution
Predict the response You may keep the correlated predictors if prediction accuracy is good. The model may still be hard to interpret.
Interpret individual coefficients Consider removing one of the redundant predictors, combining predictors, or using subject-matter judgment to keep the more meaningful variable. Do not remove variables only because their p-values are large; multicollinearity may be inflating those p-values.
Reduce dimension Use a method such as principal component regression to replace the original predictors with fewer components. The new predictors are combinations of the original variables, so coefficients become less directly interpretable.
Handle many correlated predictors Consider a regularization method such as ridge regression or lasso. These methods introduce bias intentionally to improve stability and prediction.

The key question is not just “Are the VIFs high?” but “What do we need this model to do?”

14.1.2 Effects on Inferences

Recall from Equation 13.3 that we can obtain the variance of the least squares estimators with the diagonal of \({\bf s}^{2}\left[{\bf b}\right]\).

It can be shown that this variance can be expressed in terms of VIF. So the variance of \(b_{j}\) can be expressed as \[ \begin{align} s^{2}\left[b_{j}\right] & =MSE\frac{VIF_{j}}{\left(n-1\right)\widehat{Var}\left[x_{j}\right]} \end{align} \tag{14.2}\] where \(\widehat{Var}\left[x_{j}\right]\) is the sample variance of \(x_j\).

So we see that if \(VIF_{j}\) is large (meaning there is multicollinearity when \(x_{j}\) is included in the model) then the standard error will be larger.

Noting that the test statistic for testing \(\beta_{j}=0\) in Equation 13.5 is \[ \begin{align*} t^{*} & =\frac{b_{j}}{s\left[b_{j}\right]} \end{align*} \]

So an inflated standard error \(s\left[b_{j}\right]\) will lead to a smaller \(t\) and thus a larger p-value. This will cause us to conclude there is not enough evidence for the alternative hypothesis when in fact \(\beta_{j}\ne0\).

14.1.3 Effects on CI and PI for the Response

As stated above, multicollinearity does not affect the confidence interval for the mean response or the prediction interval.

We will illustrate this with the bodyfat data below.

14.2 Standardizing predictor variables

Predictors in a regression model are often measured in very different units. One might be a skinfold thickness in millimeters and another a percentage, and their magnitudes can differ by orders of magnitude. Rescaling them to a common scale makes coefficient estimates more stable numerically and makes some methods, PCA among them, behave sensibly.

The most common rescaling is standardization: each predictor is shifted and rescaled so that it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. In this section we compare standardization with normalization and look at what standardizing does (and does not do) when predictors are correlated.

14.2.1 Standardization vs. normalization

People sometimes use “standardization” and “normalization” as if they meant the same thing, but they are different operations. Standardization transforms a variable \(X\) with

\[ z_i = \frac{x_i - \bar{x}}{s_x} \]

where \(z_i\) is the standardized value, \(x_i\) is the original value, \(\bar{x}\) is the sample mean, and \(s_x\) is the sample standard deviation. The transformed variable has mean 0 and standard deviation 1. It does not follow a normal distribution unless the original variable did; standardizing changes the location and scale, not the shape.

Normalization usually means rescaling a variable to a fixed range, often \([0, 1]\):

\[ x' = \frac{x_i - \text{min}(x)}{\text{max}(x) - \text{min}(x)} \]

Normalization is used when an algorithm needs its inputs in a bounded range, as many neural networks do. In linear models we usually standardize instead, since centering and scaling by the standard deviation gives coefficients a “per standard deviation” interpretation.

14.2.2 Why tidymodels uses the term “normalize”

In tidymodels, the recipe step that standardizes is called step_normalize(). It computes the mean and standard deviation of each predictor and then subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation, which is standardization in the sense above. The name follows a convention in some software, where both operations are loosely called normalization. In this book, when you see step_normalize(), read it as “standardize.”

14.2.3 Standardization and multicollinearity

Putting every predictor on the same scale removes large differences in magnitude between variables, and this helps numerically, particularly for methods other than least squares. Standardizing does not change the correlations among the predictors, though, so it does nothing to the multicollinearity itself. When multicollinearity is severe, we need a method that changes the predictors, such as principal component regression.

ExampleWorked example: Standardizing does not remove correlation

Standardization changes the scale of the predictors. The correlations between them stay the same.

bodyfat_predictors <- dat |>
  select(tri, thigh, midarm)

bodyfat_standardized <- bodyfat_predictors |>
  mutate(across(everything(), ~ as.numeric(scale(.x))))

14.3 Principal component regression

One way to deal with multicollinearity is principal component regression (PCR), which combines principal component analysis (PCA) with linear regression. PCR replaces the original predictors with a new set of uncorrelated variables, called principal components (PCs), and then uses some or all of those components as the predictors in the regression.

14.3.1 Introduction to principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA looks for the directions in the predictor space along which the data vary the most. The first principal component is the direction with the largest variance. The second is the direction with the largest remaining variance among all directions perpendicular to the first, and so on. Each principal component is a linear combination of the original predictors, and the components are uncorrelated with one another.

Because the last few components often carry very little of the variation, PCA can also reduce the number of variables we work with. This is most useful when there are many predictors and several of them are highly correlated.

ImportantPCA does not know about the response variable

PCA uses only the predictor variables. It finds directions that explain variation in the predictors, and those are not necessarily the directions that predict the response well.

So the first few principal components can explain most of the variation in the predictors and still not be the most useful components for predicting \(y\). Choose the number of components with model performance, subject-matter judgment, or a validation procedure, not with predictor variance alone.

TipFor those who want to see the math

Principal components are linear transformations of the original predictors chosen to capture as much variance as possible. They are computed in five steps.

  1. Standardize the data. Given \(p\) predictors \(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_p\) measured on \(n\) observations, standardize each predictor to have mean 0 and standard deviation 1:

\[ z_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij} - \bar{x}_j}{s_j} \]

where \(z_{ij}\) is the standardized value of the \(j\)th predictor for the \(i\)th observation, \(\bar{x}_j\) is the mean of the \(j\)th predictor, and \(s_j\) is its standard deviation. Without this step, the predictor with the largest units would dominate the first component.

  1. Compute the covariance matrix. For the standardized predictors, the covariance matrix is

\[ \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n-1} \mathbf{Z}^T \mathbf{Z} \]

where \(\mathbf{Z}\) is the \(n \times p\) matrix of standardized data. Since the columns are standardized, \(\mathbf{S}\) is also the correlation matrix of the original predictors.

  1. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Solve the eigenvalue problem

\[ \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}_j = \lambda_j \mathbf{v}_j \]

where \(\lambda_j\) is the \(j\)th eigenvalue and \(\mathbf{v}_j\) is its eigenvector, also called the loading vector. The eigenvalue \(\lambda_j\) is the variance of the \(j\)th principal component, and the eigenvector gives the direction of the \(j\)th new axis.

  1. Construct the principal components. The \(j\)th principal component is a linear combination of the standardized predictors:

\[ PC_j = v_{j1} z_1 + v_{j2} z_2 + \cdots + v_{jp} z_p \]

where \(v_{jk}\) is the \(k\)th element of \(\mathbf{v}_j\). The first component, PC1, has the largest possible variance. PC2 has the largest remaining variance subject to being uncorrelated with PC1, and the pattern continues through all \(p\) components.

  1. Compute the explained variance. The proportion of the total variance explained by the \(j\)th component is

\[ \text{Explained Variance of } PC_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sum_{k=1}^p \lambda_k} \]

and the cumulative proportion explained by the first \(m\) components is

\[ \text{Cumulative Explained Variance} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^m \lambda_j}{\sum_{k=1}^p \lambda_k}. \]

14.3.2 A picture of what principal components do

With two predictors we can draw the whole process. The example below uses only tri and thigh from the bodyfat data, since these two have the strongest correlation.

PCA does not move the points. It rotates the axes so that the first axis lies along the direction of greatest spread. If we plot the same 20 people using their PC scores as coordinates, the cloud is turned so that PC1 runs horizontally, and the correlation between the two new variables is 0.

The right panel shows why PCR removes multicollinearity. The new axes are uncorrelated by construction, and almost all of the variation is in the horizontal direction. If we dropped PC2 we would keep about 96% of the variation in the two predictors with a single variable.

With three or more predictors the idea is the same, although we can no longer draw it: PCA rotates the axes of the \(p\)-dimensional predictor space so that PC1 points along the longest direction of the cloud, PC2 along the longest direction perpendicular to PC1, and so on.

The loadings are the recipe for each component. A person’s PC score is a weighted sum of their standardized measurements, and the loadings are the weights. For tri and thigh, PC1 is \(0.71\,z_{tri} + 0.71\,z_{thigh}\) and PC2 is \(-0.71\,z_{tri} + 0.71\,z_{thigh}\). Someone who is one standard deviation above average on both measurements has a PC1 score of about 1.41 and a PC2 score of 0. Someone who is one standard deviation above average on thigh but exactly average on tri has scores of about 0.71 on both. So PC1 measures overall size: how much skinfold a person has at the triceps and thigh combined. PC2 measures shape: whether a person carries relatively more at the thigh than at the triceps. Since the two measurements rise and fall together for almost everyone, people differ mostly in size, and PC2 has very little variance. Three things help when reading any table of loadings. A loading near 0 means that variable plays almost no part in the component. Variables whose loadings have the same sign move the score in the same direction. And the sign of a whole component is arbitrary, since flipping every loading in a component gives the same axis pointing the other way; only the signs relative to each other mean anything.

In regression, instead of regressing the response on the original predictors, we use the principal components as the predictors. Using only the first few components keeps most of the variation in the predictors while removing the multicollinearity.

Look at the pairs of variables that measure nearly the same thing. Least squares gives educationMale and educationFemale large slopes of opposite sign (about 14.3 and -18.6), and it does the same with illiteracyMale and illiteracyFemale; the two slopes in each pair mostly cancel. PCR gives the two variables in each pair slopes with the same sign and similar size, because they load almost identically on the components it kept. The PCR slopes are biased, since we discarded six components, but they are much more stable. They still describe a change in one standardized predictor with the others held fixed, so they should be read with the same care as in Example 14.1.

PCR lets us fit a regression model even when the multicollinearity is extreme, and using only the first few PCs also reduces the dimension of the problem. It does not reduce the number of measurements we need. Every PC is a linear combination of all the original predictors, so we still have to collect every one of them. If the question is whether a variable that is hard or expensive to measure can be dropped, PCR will not answer it. We also lose the direct interpretation of the coefficients.

WarningPCR trades interpretation for stability

PCR reduces multicollinearity because the principal components are uncorrelated. The cost is that the coefficients are no longer slopes for the original predictors. The coefficient for PC1, for example, is the change in the expected response for a one-unit increase in the first principal component, not for a one-unit increase in tri, thigh, or midarm.

For this reason PCR is most appealing when prediction or numerical stability is the goal, rather than explaining the effect of each original predictor.

NoteBridge to ridge regression

PCR handles multicollinearity by changing the predictors: it replaces them with principal components, which reduces the dimension and removes the correlation, at the price of coefficients that describe components instead of the original variables. Ridge regression, discussed in Chapter 19, keeps the original predictors in the model and adds a penalty that shrinks their coefficient estimates toward 0. That often makes the estimates more stable and improves prediction when predictors are correlated, but the estimates are biased on purpose.

Both methods are useful when ordinary least squares becomes unstable. Neither one tells us which original variable is “the one true cause”; that question still requires study design, context, and careful interpretation.

14.4 Recap

In this chapter we looked at what happens when predictors overlap with each other and how principal component regression can deal with that overlap.

Idea Meaning
Multicollinearity Two or more predictors contain redundant information because they are correlated with each other.
Partial slope interpretation A regression coefficient describes the effect of changing one predictor while holding the others fixed. This is awkward when the predictors naturally move together.
VIF The variance inflation factor measures how well one predictor can be explained by the other predictors.
Large VIF The standard error for that coefficient is inflated because of multicollinearity.
Prediction vs. coefficient inference Multicollinearity can leave predictions largely unchanged while making individual coefficient tests unstable.
Standardization Rescaling a predictor to have mean 0 and standard deviation 1. It changes the scale but not the correlations among predictors.
PCA A rotation of the predictor axes that replaces correlated predictors with uncorrelated principal components, ordered by how much predictor variance they explain. PCA ignores \(y\).
PCR Regression on principal components. It can make the fit more stable, but the coefficients are harder to interpret.
Ridge regression A regularization method that keeps the original predictors and shrinks their coefficients to make the fit more stable.

14.5 Check your understanding

NoteProblems

  1. Why can a model with severe multicollinearity still make useful predictions?

  2. What does a high VIF tell us about a predictor’s relationship with the other predictors?

  3. Explain why multicollinearity tends to increase p-values for individual coefficient tests.

  4. Why is the phrase “holding all other predictors constant” especially important when discussing multicollinearity?

  5. Does standardizing predictors remove multicollinearity? Explain.

  6. In tidymodels, why might step_normalize() be a confusing name in a regression course?

  7. Why does using principal components remove multicollinearity among the transformed predictors?

  8. Why might PCR be a poor choice if the main research goal is to explain the unique effect of each original predictor?

  9. Why might the first principal component not be the best single predictor of the response?

  10. How does ridge regression respond to multicollinearity differently than PCR?

  11. In Example 14.4, the eighth principal component explained less than 1% of the variation in the predictors but raised the adjusted \(R^2\) noticeably. How can that happen?

TipSolutions

  1. Prediction uses the combined information in the predictors. If two predictors overlap, the model can still use their shared information to predict the response well. The difficulty is in separating the unique contribution of each predictor, not in prediction.

  2. The predictor can be predicted from the other predictors. A high VIF means that when we regress this predictor on the remaining predictors, \(R_i^2\) is large. In plain language, the predictor carries information that is already in the model.

  3. The test statistic for a coefficient is the estimate divided by its standard error. Multicollinearity inflates the standard error, so the test statistic shrinks and the p-value grows, even when the predictor is related to the response.

  4. If two predictors usually move together, it may be hard to imagine increasing one while the other stays fixed. That makes the partial slope hard to interpret as a real-world effect.

  5. No. Standardizing changes each predictor’s units so it has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, but the correlations stay the same. Multicollinearity is about the relationships among predictors, so standardizing alone does not remove it.

  6. It performs standardization. In this chapter, standardization means subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation, and step_normalize() does exactly that, even though some courses use the word normalization only for rescaling values to a range such as 0 to 1.

  7. The components are constructed to be uncorrelated. PCA rotates the predictor axes to new axes that are perpendicular to one another, as in the rotation plot for tri and thigh. Since the components have no linear overlap, their VIFs are 1 when they are used together in a linear model.

  8. PCR changes the predictors. The coefficients describe principal components, not the original variables. Each component combines all of the original predictors, so statements like “holding thigh and midarm fixed, a one-unit increase in tri…” are much harder to make.

  9. PCA looks only at the predictors. The first principal component is the direction with the most variation among the predictors, but nothing guarantees that this is the direction most related to \(y\).

  10. PCR replaces the predictors with uncorrelated principal components and regresses on those. Ridge regression keeps the original predictors and shrinks their coefficient estimates to make the model more stable when the predictors are correlated.

  11. PCA orders the components by how much of the predictors’ variance they carry and never looks at the response. A component with very little spread in the predictors can still be the direction that infant mortality depends on, so leaving it out costs some fit even though it carries little predictor variance.
